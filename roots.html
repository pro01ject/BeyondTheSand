Chapter One

Our Roots

Heritage, traditions and the values that travel from one generation to the next — not through books, but through hands.

A family is a landscape of its own — shaped by what came before it. In the Emirates, heritage lives in the hands that shape clay, the soil that feeds a farm, the recipes carried from one kitchen to the next, and the words repeated until they become identity.

Roots are not monuments. They are small, repeated acts: a technique relearned from an elder's memory, a harvest shared across three generations, a value practised long after the reason for it is forgotten — until it simply becomes who we are.

Story · Heritage in practice

The Pottery That Brought Them Together

The Alshemaili tribe of Ras Al Khaimah produced functional and decorative pottery from mountain clay for centuries — until modern alternatives quietly ended the craft in the 1960s. What survived was memory: the names of vessels, the sources of clay, the locations of old kilns in Wadi Haqheel.

A group of cousins decided to recover it. Too young to have learned from the craftsmen themselves, they relearned everything from their elders' stories — asking questions, experimenting with clay, visiting the surviving kilns. As one of them put it, the craft depends on cooperation, just as it always did: every potter once had someone assisting, and firing days gathered whole communities around the kiln.

Their hope is not only to revive pottery-making, but to ensure the next generation can learn it — before the last memory of how it was done fades entirely.

“What we inherit is never only an object or a place. It is a way of paying attention — and a promise to pass it on.”

Story · A farm across generations

The Ostrich Farm and Its Many Stories

Some family farms change their animals but never their heart. In Al Ain, the Alketbi family's land has been cared for since the days of camels, sheep and goats — a way of life rooted in the falaj irrigation systems of Al Hili Oasis, where the narrator's grandfather once worked for the municipality.

When ostriches arrived, the farm evolved without letting go of its foundations: nearly every part of the bird finds a use, from meat to feathers to eggs. Today around two thousand birds share the land with the next generation — grandchildren who grew up comfortable holding animals most adults would avoid.

What stays constant is the mindset: care for the land as something that asks a person to be inventive. Each generation translates the same inherited creativity differently — farming, business, art — while remaining rooted in the same soil.

How a tradition travels

  1. I

    An elder knows

    The craft, the recipe or the story lives in someone who learned it by doing — often without ever writing it down.

  2. II

    A child watches

    Knowledge passes informally: at the workbench, at the stove, beside the field. Children absorb it before they understand it.

  3. III

    A child tries

    Mistakes are part of the inheritance. The first pot leaks, the first dish burns — and the elder says try again.

  4. IV

    A child teaches

    One day the roles reverse. The tradition survives not because it was preserved, but because it was shared.

Why preserving roots matters

Identity with depth

Children who know where their family comes from — its crafts, foods and stories — grow up with a steadier sense of self.

Skills that endure

Relearning a craft keeps practical knowledge alive: how to read the land, tend the falaj, judge the clay.

A future that remembers

Every tradition handed forward is a message to the next generation: you come from somewhere, and you belong.
